01 / The big picture

What is the history of computing?

Computing history is the story of humans finding better ways to represent information, perform calculations, follow instructions, and solve problems.

🧮

Calculate

Early tools helped people count, measure, keep records, and solve arithmetic problems more reliably.

⚙️

Automate

Mechanical and electronic machines began carrying out instructions faster than people could do by hand.

✦

Connect and learn

Modern computers share information, recognize patterns, and help people explore complex questions.

One important idea: A computer is not just a screen. It is a system that takes input, processes information according to instructions, and produces output. Many computers also store information for later use.
02 / Milestones

An interactive timeline

Move from ancient counting tools to modern artificial intelligence. Select a time period to narrow the timeline.

ANCIENT WORLD · c. 2400 BCE onward

Counting boards and the abacus

People use beads, counters, and place-value methods to make arithmetic easier. The abacus develops in several forms across different regions.

1642

Blaise Pascal builds the Pascaline

Pascal designs a mechanical calculator that uses wheels and gears to add and subtract numbers.

1670s

Leibniz improves mechanical calculation

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz develops a stepped-drum calculator design intended to perform multiplication and division as well as addition and subtraction.

1801

Jacquard loom uses punched cards

Punched cards control weaving patterns. The idea of instructions represented by holes in cards later influences computing.

1830s

Charles Babbage designs the Analytical Engine

Babbage imagines a programmable mechanical machine with parts for calculation, memory, and control. It is not completed in his lifetime.

1840s

Ada Lovelace writes an algorithm

Lovelace publishes notes describing how the Analytical Engine could follow a sequence of operations. She is often recognized as an early computer programmer.

1890

Hollerith tabulating system

Herman Hollerith's punched-card machines help process data for the 1890 U.S. census, speeding up large-scale tabulation.

1930s–1940s

Electronic computing takes shape

Researchers develop electromechanical and electronic machines for scientific, military, and administrative calculations.

1940s

Vacuum-tube computers

Early electronic computers use vacuum tubes to switch and amplify electrical signals. They are large, hot, and power-hungry.

1947

The transistor is demonstrated

The transistor offers a smaller, more reliable alternative to vacuum tubes and becomes a foundation of later electronics.

1950s–1960s

Transistors and integrated circuits

Computers become more compact and dependable as transistors and then integrated circuits replace many separate components.

1970s

Microprocessors and personal computing

Putting a computer's central processing functions on a chip helps make smaller, more affordable computers possible.

1980s–1990s

Personal computers and the internet expand

Computers become common in homes, schools, and offices, while networked communication grows rapidly.

2000s–2010s

Mobile and cloud computing

Smartphones, wireless networks, and cloud services make computing portable and connected.

2020s–today

AI becomes part of everyday computing

Machine-learning systems support translation, image recognition, recommendations, scientific research, and generative tools.

03 / Five generations

How computers changed, generation by generation

The generations are a useful way to organize major changes in computer hardware. The dates are approximate because technologies overlapped.

1010
FIRST GENERATION · c. 1940s–1950s

Vacuum-tube computers

These early electronic computers used vacuum tubes to process electrical signals. They could perform calculations much faster than manual methods, but often occupied entire rooms and required substantial electricity and cooling.

Main technologyVacuum tubes
SizeRoom-sized systems
ProgrammingMachine language, wiring, and punched cards
ChallengeHeat, cost, and reliability
Remember: each generation did not instantly replace the previous one. Different kinds of computers continued to use different technologies.

05 / Instructions for computers

Programming languages: from bits to readable code

Hardware performs operations, but software gives it instructions. Programming languages help people express those instructions.

Machine language

Low-level

Instructions represented as binary patterns that a processor can execute directly.

01001000 01101001

Assembly language

Low-level

Uses short symbolic instructions that correspond closely to processor operations.

LOAD R1, 5
ADD  R1, 3

FORTRAN

1950s

Created for scientific and engineering calculations; its name comes from “Formula Translation.”

PRINT *, "HELLO, WORLD!"

COBOL

1950s

Designed for business data processing, with a style intended to be more English-like.

DISPLAY "HELLO, WORLD".

C

1970s

A powerful general-purpose language widely used for operating systems and software.

#include <stdio.h>
int main(void) {
  printf("Hello, world!");
  return 0;
}

Python

1990s

A readable, general-purpose language used in education, automation, data science, and AI.

print("Hello, world!")
06 / Computing today

Artificial intelligence: new possibilities, real responsibilities

AI systems can identify patterns in data and generate predictions or content. They can be useful, but their results need human judgment.

🧠

Machine learning

Computer systems learn patterns from examples rather than relying only on rules written one by one.

💬

Language tools

AI can help translate, summarize, answer questions, and generate text—but it can also make mistakes.

🔬

Research and discovery

Computing helps researchers analyze large datasets, model complex systems, and test ideas.

Potential benefits

  • Can assist with repetitive or time-consuming tasks.
  • Can help people analyze large amounts of information.
  • Can improve accessibility through tools such as speech recognition and translation.
  • Can support scientific research and creative exploration.

Risks and questions

  • AI can produce incorrect or misleading information.
  • Biased training data can lead to unfair outcomes.
  • Personal data and privacy need protection.
  • People must consider how automation affects jobs and society.
Think critically: AI output should be checked, especially when accuracy, safety, fairness, or important decisions are involved. Human oversight remains important.
